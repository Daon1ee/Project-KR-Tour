# Korean History Game for Kids: Concept and Value Proposition Canvas

Oct 6, 2026 · @Daon

## Project overview

This project reshapes the VR Seoul Tour concept into a game that young Korean-American children can play to meet Korean landmarks and their stories, including the sad parts of Korean history, without fear.

### Original concept: VR Seoul Tour

**Original HMW:** give people with limited cultural or educational access to Korean history a low-barrier, embodied way to connect with that history and their own identity, where the erased and restored layers sit behind familiar cultural sites.

**Original audience:** immigrant communities, so that Korea's invisible histories become known through casual, low-barrier sightseeing.

**Background.** Seoul has many well-known tourist destinations, each with a depth of history that is not visible on the surface. Gwanghwamun, the main gate of the Joseon royal palace, is widely recognized as a symbol of "K-culture" tourism, a backdrop for hanbok photography and a highlight of modern Korean travel. Beneath that polished image is a more complex history: many of Seoul's landmarks were dismantled, displaced, or destroyed during Japanese colonial rule and the Korean War, and restored decades later through prolonged national effort. These sites embody a citywide process of cultural erasure and recovery.

**Original form.** An immersive VR experience that places the user in present-day Seoul as a tourist. Through keyboard-based interaction, the user navigates the city and interacts with specific objects and spaces, which triggers shifts into past timeframes (the colonial period and wartime) that reveal the histories hidden behind each site's contemporary image.

**Three problems addressed:**

1. The difficulty of sharing culture beyond surface-level representation.
2. Limited educational access to historical context in casual or tourist encounters.
3. The broader challenge of historical recognition, especially for histories that have been actively erased or suppressed.

### The reframe: a game for children

The new target users are the owner's nieces: second-generation Korean-American children, each turning 6, with limited opportunities to learn about their ethnic identity. They are scared of dark backgrounds in stories, and even of the city rat and countryside rat fable. The goal is to let them make easy, fun contact with Korea's sad history.

That changes the design problem. A time-travel structure that moves from bright present-day Seoul into dark colonial and wartime scenes would frighten this audience. The history stays, but how it is shown, who guides the child, and what the child does inside it all change.

**Proposed working HMW (a suggestion to refine):** How might we give young Korean-American children a joyful, low-fear first encounter with the stories behind Korean landmarks, so that identity feels like something to be curious about?

## The framework: Value Proposition Canvas

The Value Proposition Canvas maps one customer segment against what you offer and checks whether the two fit. This document uses the Strategyzer (Osterwalder) version for the main canvases and keeps a Thomson-style draft in the appendix.

### Two versions of the canvas

| | Strategyzer (Osterwalder) | Peter Thomson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer side | Customer Jobs, Pains, Gains | Wants, Needs, Fears, Substitutes |
| Offer side | Products & Services, Pain Relievers, Gain Creators | Features, Benefits, Experience |
| Emphasis | Jobs-to-be-done, fit between pains/gains and the offer | Emotional and rational drivers, behavioral psychology, the felt experience |
| Rule of use | Describe one customer segment per canvas; fit is a claim until customers confirm it | Map the aggregate wants, needs and fears of everyone involved when buyer and user differ |
| Best for | A standard, official format for coursework or presentations | Experience-centered projects, since it has a dedicated Experience box |

### How each box works (Strategyzer)

- **Customer Jobs:** what the customer is trying to get done.
- **Pains:** frustrations, risks and obstacles met along the way.
- **Gains:** outcomes and benefits the customer wants, expects, or would be delighted by.
- **Products & Services:** what is offered.
- **Pain Relievers:** how the offer removes specific pains.
- **Gain Creators:** how the offer produces specific gains.

Each Pain Reliever should connect to a listed Pain, and each Gain Creator to a listed Gain. An unconnected feature is probably not needed; an important pain or gain with no matching item is a gap in the design.

### How each box works (Thomson)

- **Features:** factual description of how the product works.
- **Benefits:** what the product does for the customer, by raising pleasure or lowering pain.
- **Experience:** how using the product makes the customer feel.
- **Wants:** emotional drivers, things the customer wants to be, do or have.
- **Needs:** rational things the customer needs to get done, including latent needs they do not yet know about.
- **Fears:** fear of mistakes, loss or missing out; also the hidden reason people do not adopt something.
- **Substitutes:** existing behaviors and coping mechanisms, not only direct competitors. If the product is not better than what people already do, there is no real value proposition.

### Why Strategyzer here

Strategyzer is the standard format and was requested for this project. The Thomson version is still useful for the emotional side (fears and experience), so its draft is kept as an appendix.

## Target segments

The canvas needs one customer segment per profile, so the broad "immigrant communities" audience is narrowed to a child user and an adult mediator.

| Segment | Who | Role in the experience |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Child | Second-generation Korean-American, age 6 (the owner's nieces). Limited opportunities to learn their ethnic identity. Scared of dark backgrounds and even of the city rat and countryside rat fable. | The user who plays |
| Parent or relative | Parents, aunts and uncles of these children. Care about passing on identity and history, and worry about scaring the child or distorting history. | The mediator who introduces, sets up and talks about the experience |

### Why two profiles

Six-year-olds will not choose, set up or interpret the experience alone, and the adults decide what is appropriate. Thomson's guidance for cases where the buyer and the user differ is to map each person's wants, needs and fears separately. The child's profile is about fun, safety and belonging; the adult's profile is about identity transmission, accuracy and low effort.

### Broader audiences considered

Earlier options for narrowing the original immigrant-community audience included Korean-American second-generation and adoptee communities, and overseas Korean visitors to Seoul. The canvases below follow the nieces, since they are real, reachable first test users. Older children and adults remain a later layer (see the age-staged table).

## Design tension

The original concept reveals hidden history by moving from present-day Seoul into the colonial period and wartime, which will not work as is for a child who is frightened by dark backgrounds and by a rat fable.

### What changes from the original

| Element | Original VR Seoul Tour | Version for 6-year-olds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core framing | Revealing erased layers behind a tourist image | Lost and found: helping bring back what is missing |
| Past eras | Colonial period and wartime shown as shifts in time | "A long time ago" shown in the same bright, warm palette |
| User role | Tourist who discovers history | Helper who restores, accompanied by a guide |
| Emotional depth | Inside the experience | Moved to an adult-child conversation after play |
| Interaction | Keyboard navigation in VR | Very simple controls, no reading required |

### Options for resolving the tension

- **Reframe "erased" as "lost and found."** The fun part is restoring things, not witnessing destruction. The child helps bring back missing gates, roofs and objects, like a puzzle.
- **Avoid dark visuals for the past.** Show earlier eras in the same bright palette, as "a long time ago." Hard history is hinted at through absence ("this part is missing, can you find it?") rather than depicted.
- **Use a friendly guide character.** The haetae, the guardian statue at Gwanghwamun, can ask the child for help and stay with them throughout.
- **Treat emotional depth as a later layer.** Sadness is introduced gently through an adult conversation after play, not inside the experience.

### Platform note

Many VR headsets set minimum ages, often around 10 with parent-managed accounts. This should be checked against the specific headset. A screen-based version with keyboard or one-button controls may suit a 6-year-old better than a headset.

## Approach to the dark side of Korean history

For a 6-year-old, the colonial period and the Korean War are conveyed through how people kept, lost and recovered things, not through violence. The facts are not changed; the child receives the layer they can handle first.

### Seven design principles

1. **Show loss as "missing and found," not as violence.** History appears as traces left on places. For example, Gwanghwamun was moved in the 1920s, a large building was put in front of it, it was badly damaged in the Korean War, and it was restored to its place in 2010 (dates to be verified before use). That story needs no violent scenes. The child sees a gate with missing pieces and the job is to find the pieces.
2. **Avoid scary villains.** Do not personify an era as a bad character. Six-year-olds may turn "bad people came and broke it" into fear or prejudice toward a group. Describe situations instead: "a hard time," "the road was blocked." Because the children are growing up in the US, they may later have Japanese-heritage friends, so the game should separate talking about a government and an era from disliking a people.
3. **Control tone and color.** Show the past in a warm, old-picture style such as sepia or watercolor, not darkness. Use quiet or soft music. No sirens, explosions or screams. Make time transitions a page turn or a picture change, not a flash or a blackout.
4. **Make the child a helper, not a witness.** The child restores things rather than watching hard times. There is no fail state, only "try again." Every session ends in hope and achievement. The guide character (the haetae) stays beside the child.
5. **Put the heaviest content in conversation, not in the game.** After play, a short guide lets a parent, aunt or uncle ask, for example, "Why did the gate come back today?" The adult sets the depth by the child's reaction. If the child wants more, the adult tells more.
6. **Stage content by age.** See the table below.
7. **Check with real children and parents.** Test a prototype to see whether a gate with missing pieces is acceptable, and ask the parents what they want their children to know and when. Families differ in how and when they share history, and parent expectations set the content level.

### Age-staged content

| Stage | Age | How history is expressed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stage 1 | 5 to 7 | A gate with missing pieces, restoration puzzles, the haetae as companion |
| Stage 2 | 8 to 10 | Old photographs and people's stories, a simple explanation of why things were moved |
| Stage 3 | Teens and adults | The original time-shift experience with more direct history |

The realistic plan for the nieces is to complete Stage 1 and keep Stages 2 and 3 as expansion paths, so the original project concept is not abandoned.

## Canvas 1: Child (age 6, Korean-American, second generation)

The child wants safe, playful discovery, and Korean history arrives as a by-product of play. All pains and gains are hypotheses until confirmed with the children and their parents.

### Customer profile

| Box | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Customer Jobs** | Play and explore. Spend fun time with family. Make sense of "Korean" things around them (food, language, places). Feel like they belong. |
| **Pains** | Scared of dark or scary scenes (even the city rat and countryside rat fable). Cannot read or follow complex instructions. Gets bored quickly. Feels lost without words or context. Does not want to be alone in an unfamiliar place. Resists anything that feels like studying. |
| **Gains: required** | Feels safe and in control. |
| **Gains: expected** | Simple controls. A friendly guide. |
| **Gains: desired** | Gets to help or win. Collects rewards. Laughs. Shows family what they found. |
| **Gains: unexpected** | Naturally learns Korean places, characters and stories through play. Feels that Korea is fun and a story that belongs to them. Asks to go back, or asks about Korea later. |

### Value map

| Box | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Products & Services** | Bright, playful Seoul. Landmark missions, starting with Gwanghwamun. A friendly guardian guide (the haetae). Restoration puzzles ("find the missing piece"). Warm, storybook-style "long ago" scenes with no dark palette. Short sessions of 3 to 5 minutes. Simple controls, no reading required. |
| **Pain Relievers** | No dark, loud or violent scenes. Loss is shown as missing or moved pieces, never as destruction. Transitions use page-turn effects, not blackouts. The guide stays with the child throughout. Voice and visual cues instead of text. No fail state, only "try again." No villain characters. |
| **Gain Creators** | The child restores a gate, roof or object and gets a reward. Stamps or stickers per landmark. The guide celebrates each success. Learning is embedded in play (landmark names, characters, "what was missing"), never quizzes. A "show family" moment at the end. |

| Does not want to be alone | Guide character stays beside the child |

| Child gain | Gain creator that delivers it |
| --- | --- |

## Canvas 2: Parent or relative (mediator)

The adult wants to pass on identity and history without scaring the child, and has little time to prepare. This profile shares the product with Canvas 1 but has its own value map.

### Customer profile

| Box | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Customer Jobs** | Pass Korean identity and history to children. Find age-appropriate cultural content. Spend meaningful time with kids. Answer kids' questions about Korea, often without formal knowledge. |
| **Pains** | Children's Korean history material is scarce, and often too heavy or too shallow. Kids resist "studying." Fear of scaring the child, oversimplifying, or causing prejudice toward a group of people. Limited time to prepare. Feels unqualified to explain history. |
| **Gains: required** | Content is safe and accurate. |
| **Gains: expected** | Easy setup. |
| **Gains: desired** | A shared activity with the child. The child shows curiosity about Korea. The child actually learns about Korean history and identity. |
| **Gains: unexpected** | The child raises questions on their own, opening a natural conversation about harder history. |

### Value map

| Box | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Products & Services** | The same child experience, plus a short parent guide with conversation prompts. An optional "grown-up layer" after play: a brief, factual explanation of what happened. Age-staged content (5 to 7 first; 8 to 10 and teens and up as later layers). |
| **Pain Relievers** | Age-appropriate design removes the fear of scaring the child. The game teaches without preparation. Content is fact-checked behind the simplification. History is told through situations (moved, blocked, restored) rather than blaming people, which lowers the risk of prejudice. Parent prompts fill the "I do not know how to explain this" gap. |
| **Gain Creators** | Natural conversation starters ("What did the haetae ask you to find?"). A shared adult-child activity. Depth the adult can adjust to the child's reaction. A path to fuller history as the child grows. |

### Fit links to check

| Parent pain | Pain reliever that answers it |
| --- | --- |
| Fear of scaring the child | Age-appropriate design, no dark scenes |
| No time to prepare | Self-explanatory play, simple setup |
| Feels unqualified to explain | Conversation prompts in the parent guide |
| Worry about prejudice or distortion | Situation-based storytelling, fact-checked content |

## Understanding Gains, and learning history as a gain

Gains are the outcomes and benefits a customer wants, expects, or would be delighted by; they are the positive counterpart to Pains. Learning Korean history is a real gain for these children, but it is written as a by-product of play, not as something the child asks for.

### Gains versus the other boxes

- **Customer Jobs** are what the customer is trying to get done.
- **Pains** are what goes wrong or gets in the way.
- **Gains** are what success looks like, or what would make it even better.

### Four kinds of gains

| Kind | Meaning | Example in this project |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Required | The basics; without them the solution does not work | The experience is safe for a 6-year-old |
| Expected | Things the customer assumes they will get | Simple controls, a friendly guide |
| Desired | Things they would like but do not strictly expect | A collectible sticker for each landmark |
| Unexpected | Things they would not think to ask for but would love | The child asks to visit Korea to see the real gate |

Gains can also be functional (saves time), emotional (feels proud), social (connects with family) or about cost savings.

### How to write a gain

Write gains from the customer's point of view, as outcomes rather than features. "Has a guardian character" is a feature and belongs in Products & Services. "Feels accompanied and safe" is the gain that the character creates. Every Gain Creator should point to a listed Gain.

### Can a 6-year-old gain Korean history?

Yes. Because the game is set in Korea, the child naturally picks up Korean places, characters and stories by playing. On the canvas, the point is wording: a 6-year-old does not wake up wanting to learn Korean history, so the gain is written at the level the child can take away and framed as something that follows from play.

Examples of child-level gains:

- Knows that the haetae guards the big gate in Seoul.
- Knows that a long time ago the gate was broken and people worked to bring it back.
- Recognizes Gwanghwamun in a photo or in real life.
- Feels that Korea has stories that belong to them.

**Where it goes on the canvas:**

- **Child profile, Gains (unexpected):** naturally gets to know Korean places and characters through play, and feels Korea is fun and a story that belongs to them.
- **Parent profile, Gains (desired):** the child actually learns about Korean history and identity. The adult states this goal directly.

**Design implication.** Because the child is not seeking history, the learning is embedded in play, not added on top. A quiz or a text panel would turn the game into studying, which the children resist. "Help the haetae find the missing roof tiles" teaches the same content as an action, so the Gain Creators are play mechanics such as restoration puzzles and character missions.

## Fit hypotheses to test

Strategyzer treats fit as a claim until customers confirm it, so every match drawn above is a hypothesis. The nieces and their parents are the first evidence; a few more interviews would strengthen it.

| # | Hypothesis | How to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Bright "missing piece" design relieves the child's fear of dark content | Show a rough prototype (even slides) and observe the nieces' reactions, including what makes them laugh and what makes them pull away |
| 2 | Self-explanatory play relieves the parent's lack of preparation time | Ask the parents directly whether they would use it without setup |
| 3 | Play-embedded learning produces recall and curiosity afterward | A few days later, ask the child what they remember about the places and whether they ask questions |
| 4 | Parents are comfortable with the amount of history included at each stage | Ask what they want their children to know, and when |
| 5 | A gate with missing pieces is not frightening to a 6-year-old | Prototype test before building the full level |

Identity connection without forcing is the riskiest gain because it is hard to measure. The recall and follow-up questions in hypothesis 3 are the closest available signal.

## Appendix: Thomson canvas draft

This earlier draft uses Thomson's boxes. It is more emotional than the Strategyzer version and is useful for the Fears and Experience content. It was written before the "lost and found" and staging decisions were finalized.

### Child profile

| Box | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Wants** | To play, to be with family, to feel proud of something "Korean" that is fun, to have a friend or character |
| **Needs** | Short sessions, simple controls, bright and safe visuals, guidance from an adult nearby |
| **Fears** | Dark scenes, scary characters, being alone, getting stuck or doing it wrong |
| **Substitutes** | Picture books, Korean cartoons and YouTube, family stories, weekend Korean school, games |

### Parent or relative profile

| Box | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Wants** | Kids who feel connected to their Korean roots, shared experiences with kids |
| **Needs** | Age-appropriate content, accurate history, easy setup, conversation starters |
| **Fears** | Scaring the child, oversimplifying or distorting history, kids losing interest, "forcing" identity |
| **Substitutes** | Children's history books, Korean school, visiting Korea, telling stories themselves |

### Product side

| Box | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Features** | Bright present-day Seoul, restoration puzzle interactions, guardian character guide, short missions per landmark, optional parent prompts |
| **Benefits** | Learns Korean landmarks through play, no scary imagery, gives adults a natural way to start a conversation |
| **Experience** | "I helped bring back something that was lost," pride, curiosity, safe closeness with family |

The Substitutes box shows what the game must beat: the existing ways these families already learn about Korea. The differentiators are a low barrier, an embodied and playful experience, and a design built for very young children.
