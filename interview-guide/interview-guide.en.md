# Interview Guide: Two 6-Year-Old Nieces and Their Parents

2026-10-07 · Daon

Korean version: [interview-guide.ko.md](interview-guide.ko.md)

## What we need to find out

This round of interviews tests the five fit hypotheses on the canvas and fills in the blanks the canvas could only guess at. The parents tell us what is safe, wanted and realistic. The children show us what they actually enjoy and what they shy away from.

| # | Hypothesis from the canvas | From whom | Method |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The bright "missing piece" design takes the edge off the child's fear of dark content | Children | Stimulus ladder and observation, plus what the parents say scares the child |
| 2 | A self-explanatory game relieves the parents' lack of preparation time | Parents | Real behavior around everyday media and cultural activities, then their reaction to the concept |
| 3 | Learning embedded in play creates memory and curiosity later | Children, a few days later | Recall check and the questions the child raises unprompted |
| 4 | Parents are satisfied with the amount of history shown at each stage | Parents | What they have told the children so far, what they want to tell, and when |
| 5 | A door with a missing piece is not scary to a 6-year-old | Children | Stimulus ladder; stop at the first sign of discomfort |

### What the canvas could only guess

- How much Korean the children understand and speak, and which language the game should use.
- What the children already know: places, characters, stories, the haetae.
- Which devices they use at home and the screen-time rules. This decides whether a VR headset, PC or tablet is realistic.
- Whether the parents actively want to nurture the children's Korean identity, and how they feel about it.
- What they already use (books, Korean school, visits to Korea, videos). The game has to beat these.

### Concept directions to settle before the prototype

This round is less about confirming hypotheses and more about listening to the targets so the prototype's direction can be narrowed. Getting an answer to the five questions below is enough.

| # | Question to settle | Options on the table | Where we find out |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Which Korean story do we start with? | The restoration of Gwanghwamun / history through objects and food such as ondol, giwa and food / a mix of both | Parent blocks 3 and 5, child activities |
| 2 | How and when do we handle painful history? | Symbolically inside the game / only in conversation outside the game / later, when the child is older | Parent block 3 |
| 3 | How do we convey painful history in a multicultural setting? | Fact-centered / peace-and-understanding-centered / postpone | Parent block 3 |
| 4 | Which format suits the children? | VR / tablet or PC screen / pop-up book / paper plus digital hybrid | Parent block 4, child activities |
| 5 | What kind of play do the children accept easily and enjoy? | Puzzle / exploration / making / character | Parent block 4, child activities |

Hypotheses 1 to 5 above (stimulus ladder and recall check) can be verified in a second round with a paper prototype, once the concept is chosen. This round, the "missing piece" activity in the child session is optional, and nothing that might upset a child is used.

## Plan and order

Interview the parents first. Their answers decide what the children may be shown and how, so the child sessions come next and the recall check comes last.

1. **Parent interview (about 60 minutes).** Ideally both parents together, at home or by video. At the end, ask permission for the child sessions.
2. **Child sessions (10 to 15 minutes per child).** One at a time or together, as the parents advise. A parent stays in the room. Play comes first and the concept comes after.
3. **Recall check (3 to 5 minutes, 3 to 7 days later).** Make it a casual check-in, not a test.
4. **Parent follow-up (10 minutes).** Ask what the children said or did afterward and whether they raised questions at home.

### Place

- Somewhere familiar to the children, such as their home, at a time when they are rested and fed.
- During the child sessions the parents stay where the children can see them. Children who feel safe give useful reactions.
- A close relative running the session makes the children comfortable, but also raises the risk that they give answers an adult will like. Phrase every question as "Can you help me?", and record a shrug or a squirm as an answer too.

### Roles

- The facilitator leads the conversation. If possible, have someone else take notes or (with consent) film, so the facilitator can focus on the children.
- The parents are interviewees in the parent interview and a helper at the side in the child sessions. Ask them beforehand not to answer for the children or steer their reactions.

## How adults and 6-year-olds differ

A 6-year-old has trouble reflecting on motives or imagining a product that does not exist yet. So in the child sessions we observe behavior and feelings, and in the parent interview we ask about past experience and reactions to the concept.

|  | Parents | 6-year-old |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Method | Conversation, then a reaction to a short concept description | Play, drawing, picking photos, trying a paper prototype |
| Time | About 60 minutes | 10 to 15 minutes, with a break in the middle and an early finish if needed |
| Question style | Open questions about real past events ("Tell me about the most recent time you...") | Very small, concrete choices ("Which one do you like best?") and show-me tasks |
| What they can tell us | Habits, worries, goals, what is realistic at home | What they enjoy, what they pull back from, what they remember |
| What they cannot tell us | What the child feels in the moment | Why they like or fear something, what they will want in future |
| Main bias risk | Giving answers that make them sound like a good parent | Pleasing the adult, saying "yes" to everything |
| How to reduce bias | Ask about behavior, not opinions, and avoid leading wording | Offer two real options, treat silence and gestures as answers, do not open with "Was it fun?" |
| Language | Whatever language the parents are most comfortable in | The language the child chooses; Korean words only as part of the play |
| Recording | Audio or notes after permission | Notes and observation; film only if the parents allow it and the child is at ease |

### Principles when working with the children

- Do not ask "why" about feelings. Say "show me", or have them choose between two faces or two pictures.
- Do not deliberately test frightening stimuli. The goal is not to find what is scary but to find the gentlest version of what works.
- Treat "I want to stop" or walking away as a clear answer.

## Parent interview guide

This interview is an exploration to settle the concept direction before building a prototype. It starts with Korean identity, moves to history, then to platform and game style, and shows the concept ideas at the end so they do not influence the earlier answers. Ask open questions and follow up with "Could you tell me a little more?" and "What happened next?"

"Direction 1 to 5" in the Purpose column refers to the numbers in the "Concept directions to settle before the prototype" table above, and "Hypothesis 1 to 5" refers to the canvas fit hypotheses. Give the parents a short break between blocks 3 and 4 so they do not get tired.

### Block 1: Warm-up (5 minutes)

| Question | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| What does a typical weekend with the children look like? | Settle in and learn real daily life |
| What do the children love most right now? | Clues for play (characters, games, activities) |
| Is there one thing the children are afraid of or dislike? | Gently open the topic of fear (Hypothesis 1) |

### Block 2: Korean identity (12 minutes). Start here and spend the most time on it

| Question | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| What Korean things do the children come across at home day to day? (food, language, holidays, videos and so on) | Current touchpoints and substitutes |
| What language do the children use with you, with each other and with their grandparents? | Decide the game language and voice guide |
| How do the children talk about Korea when they introduce themselves? | The identity the child feels for themselves |
| Tell me about the last time the children asked about Korea or being Korean. | Real curiosity and how the parents answered |
| Is there anything you are doing now so the children feel connected to Korea? How is it going? | Substitutes: books, Korean school, visits, videos |
| If you ranked the parts of Korean identity you most want to pass on, what would the order be? (language, food, holidays, history, relatives, values and so on) | Priority for which story to start with (Direction 1) |
| Is there anything burdensome or hard about nurturing that identity? Where would you like more help? | Find the discomfort the game could relieve (Hypothesis 2) |
| Have you read books about Korea with the children? Which ones, and how did they react? (picture books, folktales, history books and so on) | Exposure to Korean books and formats that work |

### Block 3: Korean history (15 minutes)

| Question | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Have you talked with the children about Korean history, including the hard parts? How did you put it, and how did it go? | Real behavior and comfort level (Hypothesis 4) |
| What kind of education about Korean history do you feel the children should get? Why? | Educational goal (Direction 1) |
| What do you most want the children to feel through history? (pride, learning, empathy, peace and so on) | The feeling and tone to convey |
| How do you decide how far you can go with a 6-year-old? | The parents' own criteria |
| How do you think you will handle dark history such as the Japanese colonial period and the Korean War with the children? Who, when and in what way? | Approach and timing for painful history (Direction 2) |
| If a game covered some of that, what way would be fine and what way would make you uncomfortable? | Boundaries inside the game (Direction 2) |
| When you imagine explaining hard history, what worries you most? | Fear of frightening or distorting |
| Do the children have friends of Japanese descent or from other cultural backgrounds? | The multicultural setting (Direction 3) |
| As Korean Americans, do you want to share the pain of Korean history with the children, or not yet? Why do you feel that way? | Willingness and motive to share the pain (Direction 3) |
| Do you worry it might affect their relationships with friends from other backgrounds? How do you handle that? | Bias concerns and coping (Direction 3) |
| Have you come across books or videos about Korean history? What worked and what did not? | What already works and what does not |

### Card sort: when to introduce each topic (5 minutes, continuing from block 3)

Have the parents place the cards below on a line. The line is divided into "around age 6 / ages 8 to 9 / ages 10 to 12 / 13 and up / not sure yet". For each card, ask "Why that age?". Treat moving a card without words as an answer too. The parents may add cards.

| Card | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Old objects such as ondol and giwa | Stories held in homes and everyday objects |
| Korean food | Stories behind food such as tteok and kimchi |
| Holidays and customs | The origins of holidays like Seollal and Chuseok |
| Palaces and kings | Palaces such as Gwanghwamun and their stories |
| Japanese colonial period | Painful history |
| Korean War | Painful history |

### Block 4: Platform and game style (15 minutes)

| Question | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| What games, apps or videos do the children enjoy most right now? Tell me the names and why they like them. | What makes it fun for them (Direction 5) |
| On which device, and how often? What are the screen-time rules? | Device and time reality |
| Do they play alone or together? How does it feel when they play together? | Potential for shared activity |
| When the children meet a new game, how do they learn it? (try it by touch, watch and copy, listen to an explanation) | Control difficulty and how to guide |
| On a screen, what action do the children get most excited about? (touching, dragging, shaking, talking and so on) | Interaction ideas (Direction 5) |
| Have the children tried VR or headset games? Do you have a headset at home? | Experience and access (Direction 4) |
| If they have, how did they react? If not, what do you think about it? | VR acceptance |
| Would you want the children to be exposed to VR? At what age would it feel okay? Do you have any worries? (eye health, dizziness, immersion that could be frightening and so on) | Willingness to expose, concerns, and checking device age limits (Direction 4) |
| If not VR, where would it feel most natural: tablet, PC or TV? | Alternative platforms (Direction 4) |
| Have you read a pop-up book with the children? What did they like about it? | What is fun about paper books (Direction 4) |
| If they saw the same story on a screen, do you think they would have similar fun? Is there anything they would miss? (the feel of touching, the fun of unfolding and so on) | Whether tactile fun is lost (Direction 4) |
| What would you think of using paper and digital together, for example holding a tablet up to a paper book to make the pictures move? | Opinion on a hybrid format (Direction 4) |

### Block 5: Reaction to the concept ideas (10 minutes)

Show the three ideas as cards only after all the earlier blocks are finished. Keep each card short: one sentence and one bright picture.

| Card | Description |
| --- | --- |
| A. Finding the missing pieces | A bright, short game where the child helps the haetae find the missing pieces of Gwanghwamun. No scary scenes, and a short guide after play helps the parents talk with the child |
| B. Korean history through objects | Meet the stories in objects like ondol, giwa and food through play |
| C. Pop-up storybook | A pop-up storybook that moves when you unfold and pull it (paper or digital) |

| Question | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| What is your first impression of the three? | Honest first impression before probing |
| If you could pick just one for the children now, which? What about three years from now? Why? | Priority and timing |
| If we introduce history through objects or food as in B, which objects do you think would resonate most with the children? Do they already meet any at home? | Material for the objects concept (Direction 1) |
| Does B feel like it avoids the dark history, which is a letdown, or is that actually good at this stage? | Acceptance of a gentler approach (Direction 2) |
| What if we combined A and B, for example finding giwa tiles to restore Gwanghwamun? | Hybrid idea |
| What would it take for you to feel comfortable letting the children play? What would be a deal breaker? | Required and expected gains for the parent |
| How do you imagine using it? The child alone, together, or when guests come over? | Shared use and self-explanatory play (Hypothesis 2) |
| If there were a guide for parents, what would you want in it? | Parent guide design |
| What would it look like for you to feel "that went well"? | The parent's definition of success |

### Block 6: Wrap-up (3 minutes)

- Did anything new come to mind while we talked?
- Is there anything I did not ask that I should have?
- May I do a short play session with the children? Is there anything you would like me to avoid?
- Is there anyone else (grandparents, teachers) who tells the children Korean stories?

### Phrasing to avoid

- Leading questions: instead of "Wouldn't it be good if...?" or "Are you worried about...?", ask "If you have any worries about...what are they?"
- Hypotheticals about the future: instead of "Would you use it?", ask what they do now.
- Judging past choices. Keep a neutral tone about what they did or did not tell the children.

## Child session guide

For a 6-year-old this session is not an interview but a short playtime with a few things to look at and do. Keep it to 10 to 15 minutes, follow the child's lead, and keep a parent in the room. Say "I'm making a game and I need your help", and do not call it a lesson about Korea.

### Order of activities

| Step | Time | What to do | What to watch or ask |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Warm-up play | 3 min | Play or draw whatever the child wants. Ask to see a favorite toy or character. | Mood, energy, the character and game they pick |
| 2. Feelings cards | 1 min | Show three faces (happy, okay, scared) and practice. "Which face is ice cream? A loud noise?" | Whether the child understands and can point to one. This becomes their way of answering later |
| 3. Meet the guide friend | 2 min | Show a friendly cartoon-style haetae. "A kind door guardian from Korea. Does he look funny or brave to you?" | First reaction, laughing, the name they give him |
| 4. The whole door | 2 min | Show a bright, intact Gwanghwamun picture. "A big gate in Seoul. Have you seen a gate like this?" | What they know, curiosity, pointing |
| 5. The missing piece (paper prototype) | 3 min | Show the same gate with one tile or one door missing, in the same bright colors. "The haetae lost a piece. Can you help find it?" Let them choose from three pieces. | Leaning in, smiling, trying, any sign of worry, whether they can do it without reading |
| 6. Happy ending | 1 min | The gate becomes whole again. The haetae gives a sticker or stamp. | Pride, whether they show a parent, whether they want to do it again |
| 7. Choosing and closing | 2 min | "Which part did you like best?" (with two pictures). Ask "How was it?" with the faces, thank them, and let them pick a sticker. | What they pick first, whether they want more |

### Concept direction activities (3 to 5 minutes, only if the child is enjoying it)

This round, steps 3 to 6 of the table above (the guide friend and the missing piece) are optional. Use the activities below to find out what the child likes. Each is a choose-from-picture-cards activity, and you drop it if the child gets bored.

| Activity | How | What to observe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pick a favorite kind of play | Show 4 picture cards (puzzle, adventure and exploring, making and stacking, caring for and dressing up a character). Say "Pick just one you most want to try" and have the child put a sticker on it. | Which card they reach for first, whether they hesitate, and what they say unprompted; do not ask why on purpose (Direction 5) |
| Pick a Korean object or food | Show 3 to 4 picture cards (a warm ondol floor, giwa tiles on a roof, food like tteok or kimchi). Ask "Which story are you most curious about?" and let them choose. | Curiosity, what they already know, connections such as "we have this at home" (Direction 1) |
| Paper book vs. screen | If you have a pop-up book, let the child handle it freely. Then show a similar moving picture or video on a phone or tablet. Ask "Which was more fun?" and have them pick one of two pictures. | How much they touch, whether they try to touch the screen, which one holds their attention longer (Direction 4) |
| Reaction to device photos | Show photo cards of a child using a tablet, a TV and a headset. Use the feeling faces to ask "Would you like to try this?" | Reaction to the headset photo: fear, curiosity or indifference (Direction 4) |

### Device safety note

Headsets have a manufacturer's recommended age that varies by device (often said to be 10 and up, so check each one), and eye strain or dizziness is also a concern for a 6-year-old. So do not let the children wear a headset in the interview. Check through photos and the parents' accounts. Only if the parents have a headset at home and explicitly allow it, check that device's age guidance and limit yourself to watching the child use it beside a parent the way they usually do.

### Stimulus ladder (one step at a time)

Stop climbing the ladder at the first sign of discomfort. Do not use anything dark, loud or showing destruction.

1. A bright, intact gate (step 4).
2. The same gate with one small piece missing, in the same colors (step 5).
3. Only if step 2 was clearly fun: a second piece missing, still in a bright storybook style.

If discomfort shows at any step, go back to the whole gate and the happy ending, and note at which step it happened. That is the answer to Hypotheses 1 and 5.

### Language

Use whichever language the child answers in. Use Korean words ("haetae", "Gwanghwamun") only as part of play, never as a vocabulary test.

### Recall check (3 to 7 days later)

Do not announce it. Bring it up in passing during an ordinary visit. Play with the stickers or ask "Do you remember our gate game?" Note what the child brings up before you ask anything.

| Question or action | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| Do not mention the game and see whether the child brings it up | Spontaneous memory and curiosity |
| "Who was the friend guarding the gate?" | Character memory (Hypothesis 3) |
| "What did that friend ask you to help with?" | Task memory (Hypothesis 3) |
| "Do you want to play again?" | Real interest rather than politeness, and how excited they are |

## Third-party interview guide (native Koreans, second-generation Korean Americans, general adults)

This guide collects an outside view from people who are neither the parents nor the children. It focuses on two things. First, how people see teaching Korean history to a 6-year-old. Second, what kind of fun and meaning is possible, and where the line is crossed, if the same subjects (the Japanese colonial period and the Korean War) are reinterpreted as a game for general adult players. Device and game-medium questions are asked in the same frame as the parent and child interviews so the answers can be compared later.

### What we want to find out

| # | Question | Where we find out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | How do they see teaching Korean history to a 6-year-old (need, timing, approach)? | Block 2, timing cards |
| 2 | When painful history is introduced to a child, what is acceptable and what is uncomfortable? | Block 2 |
| 3 | If the Japanese colonial period and the Korean War were turned into a game for general adults, what fun and meaning would be possible? | Block 3 |
| 4 | Where would it cross a line? | Block 3 |
| 5 | Which devices and game media feel natural, and could it expand to adults? | Blocks 4 and 5 |

### Whom to ask

Starting with 2 to 3 people per group is enough. Everyone is 18 or older. They may have children, but people who appeared in the parent interviews are excluded.

| Group | Why we need them | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adults who grew up in Korea (living in Korea or the US) | A view shaped by Korean history education, and a benchmark for what and how to teach a 6-year-old | Korean schooling may become the yardstick and overlook the situation of children abroad |
| Second-generation Korean Americans (adults) | People who went through the same environment as the nieces, and what helped or burdened them as children | Memories may be romanticized or mixed with present feelings |
| General adult gamers (need not be Korean; knowledge of Korean history varies) | The game's appeal and clarity, and whether it could expand to adults | Participants for whom the subject is personal, such as people of Japanese descent, join with consent and talk only as far as they are comfortable |

When selecting participants, check only three things: which group they belong to, whether they have experience looking after a child around 6, and how much they play games.

### How to run it

- One person at a time, about 40 to 45 minutes, in person or by video. It can be done before or after the parent interviews, but make sure the parents' answers do not reach the participant.
- Get consent before recording or taking notes. Tell them in advance that the topic can touch family history or personal experience, and that they may skip any question.
- Adults tend to give the "right" answer. Say there is no correct answer, and ask about experience and behavior rather than opinions.
- Do not state the facilitator's own position or judgment, and do not steer toward historical disputes or political arguments.

### Block 1: Warm-up and background (5 minutes)

| Question | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Where did you grow up, and what is your relationship to Korea? (residence, language, visits) | Confirm the group and background |
| Do you remember when and how you first learned Korean history? How did it feel? | First memory and feeling |
| What games or videos do you mainly enjoy these days? | Gaming habits and taste |

### Block 2: Korean history for a 6-year-old (12 minutes)

| Question | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| What do you think about teaching Korean history to a 6-year-old? Is it too early, or necessary? Why? | First reaction on need and timing |
| Thinking back to when and how you first learned it, how would you want it to go for the children? | Standard drawn from their own experience |
| Is there a history story from your childhood that stayed with you, or one that frightened or unsettled you? | What lasts from childhood, and risk points |
| Which stories do you think are fine to start with at age 6? (ondol, giwa, food, holidays, palaces and so on) | Which Korean story to start with (Direction 1) |
| For painful history such as the Japanese colonial period or the Korean War, at about what age, who, and how would be best? | Timing and manner for painful history (Direction 2) |
| Should a child growing up abroad meet it differently from a child growing up in Korea? What if they have Japanese friends or friends from other cultures? | Differences in a multicultural setting (Direction 3) |
| If a child met Korean history through play, is there anything good or worrying about it? Is it okay to treat it as "fun"? | View on treating it as play |
| Have you seen Korean history books, videos or games for children? What worked and what fell short? | Limits of existing content |
| (For second-generation Korean Americans) What helped most and what felt burdensome when you were learning Korean identity as a child? | Experience from the same environment as the nieces |

### Card sort: when to introduce each topic (3 minutes, continuing from block 2)

Lay out the same 6 cards and timeline used in the parent interview. Ask "Why that age?" for each card. Compare the placement with the parents' later.

### Block 3: Painful history and a game for adults (12 minutes)

Now shift the view from the 6-year-old to a general adult player. Start by making the scope clear: "if it were a game that adults play". The questions ask for views, not judgments, so skip a question right away if the participant finds it uncomfortable.

| Question | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Have you seen a film, drama, book or game about the Japanese colonial period or the Korean War? What stayed with you, and why? | What already works and what has failed |
| If this subject were made into a game, what kind of approach would be meaningful, and what would be uncomfortable? (show the method cards) | Which approaches are accepted |
| What kind of "fun" in a game could fit this subject? (story, choices, puzzle and collecting, immersive experience, learning) | Types of fun that suit the subject |
| Is there a point where the word "fun" itself feels wrong? Where is that line? | Position of the line (Direction 2) |
| What perspective matters in the story? (victims, resistance, ordinary people's daily life, reconciliation and so on) | Viewpoint and tone |
| If such a game existed, who should play it, and what feeling should it leave at the end? | Audience and the game's goal |
| If players from other cultural backgrounds, such as Japan, played it, what would worry you and what would you expect? | View on multicultural players (Direction 3) |
| Would you actually play it? Under what conditions? If a film or a book would be better, why? | The value and limits of the game medium |
| Could the gentle version for 6-year-olds and an adult version share a world or characters, such as the haetae? Or should they be separate? | Potential for expansion and brand risk |

#### Adult method cards (5 cards to show)

Show them one at a time at question 2 and ask "Which would be most meaningful, and which would be uncomfortable?" Do not show combat- or violence-centered approaches; hear opinions on them only if the participant raises them first.

| Card | Description |
| --- | --- |
| A. Story choice | A game where you follow an ordinary person's day in that era and make choices |
| B. Restoration and collection | A game where you find missing relics or places and restore them one by one (connects to the 6-year-old concept) |
| C. Puzzle and deduction | A game where you gather records and clues and reason out what happened |
| D. Immersive experience (VR) | An experience of walking through and looking around places from that era |
| E. Strategy and simulation | A game that helps you understand that era's choices through decisions and resource management (may be the most controversial, so it is shown to find the line) |

### Block 4: Devices and game media (8 minutes)

Ask in the same frame as parent block 4. Keep the order and wording as close as possible so the answers can be compared with the parents and children.

| Question | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| What game or app do you enjoy most these days? Please tell me the name and why you like it. | What makes it fun |
| On which device, and how often? | Device and time reality |
| Alone or with others? | Potential for shared play |
| Have you tried VR or headset games? How was it? (dizziness, immersion, discomfort) | VR experience and acceptance (Direction 4) |
| How would you feel if an adult walked through a historical place in VR? | Appeal and burden of adult VR |
| Could you recommend VR for a 6-year-old? At what age, and with what worries? | An outside view on VR for children (Direction 4) |
| If not VR, which of tablet, PC, TV or a paper book would feel most natural for a 6-year-old? | Alternative platforms (Direction 4) |
| Have you seen a pop-up book? Would the fun change if the same story were on a screen? What about mixing paper and digital? | Tactile fun and views on a hybrid format (Direction 4) |

### Block 5: Reaction to the child concept cards (5 minutes, optional)

Show the parent concept cards A, B and C (finding missing pieces, Korean history through objects, pop-up storybook) only after block 4. To tell them apart from the adult method cards, call them "the cards for 6-year-olds".

| Question | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| If you had to pick one for a 6-year-old, which would be best? Why? | Priority from an outside view |
| Is there anything that worries you at first sight? | Warning signs |
| Is any of these worth expanding for adults, and who would play it? | Potential for expansion |

### Block 6: Wrap-up (3 minutes)

- Did anything new come to mind while we talked?
- Is there anything I did not ask that I should have?
- Is there anyone or any content you think I should look at on this subject?

### Phrasing to avoid and cautions

- Do not steer toward one position because the facilitator is Korean. Instead of "Japan hasn't forgotten it, right?", ask "What view do you have of this subject?"
- Avoid judging phrasing. Instead of "Wouldn't a game like that be uncomfortable?", ask "Which parts feel comfortable and which feel uncomfortable?"
- If family history or experiences of harm come up, stop asking questions and make room to listen. Do not ask for more, and say again that they can skip or stop.
- If an argument over historical facts begins, do not argue about facts; return to the question: "From the game's point of view, how would that be?"

### Recording and comparing

Record one line per participant.

| Item | Participant 1 | Participant 2 | Participant 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Group (Korean / second-generation Korean American / general) |  |  |  |
| Attitude toward teaching a 6-year-old Korean history (for / conditional / against) |  |  |  |
| Card placement by age (Japanese colonial period, Korean War) |  |  |  |
| Where the line is for painful history in a game |  |  |  |
| Adult method cards chosen and avoided |  |  |  |
| Devices and media they called natural |  |  |  |
| Attitude toward VR |  |  |  |
| Concept card A/B/C chosen for the 6-year-old |  |  |  |

When comparing with the parent interviews, look at how different the card placements are, whether the parents are more cautious or more active, and whether the attitudes toward devices and VR match. Where the difference is large, ask why once more, and use the points where the two groups disagree as clues for deciding the concept direction.

## Consent and stopping rules

The children's comfort comes before the research. These rules apply to both sessions, and you should agree on them with the parents before the child sessions.

### With the parents

- Explain the purpose in two sentences, what will be recorded (notes, audio, photos) and where it will be used (for example a class project, a portfolio).
- Get clear consent before recording or filming, and before using the children's names, photos or drawings. Offer to use first names only, or to leave names out.
- Tell them they can skip any question and stop at any time, and that there are no right answers about how to raise children.
- Agree before the child sessions on what may and may not be shown.

### With the children

- Ask in simple words whether they want to play a game together. If they say no or walk away, do not persuade; stop.
- Keep a parent in the room, and let the child sit next to them if they want.
- If a child frowns, goes quiet, says they want to stop, hides or keeps looking at a parent, stop immediately and move to the happy ending.
- Do not make a child approach or touch something they have pulled back from.
- Even when you stop early, end every session with the whole picture and a sticker.

### Materials and drawings

- Keep notes and recordings private, and delete recordings once you have written up what you need.
- Ask permission before returning or keeping the children's drawings.
- Do not share observations about a specific child outside the family without the parents' permission.

## Side project memo: a digital version of the pop-up book

This memo is an idea to pursue separately from the current game project. Block 4 of the interview and the paper vs. screen comparison are the starting point. The core question is how interaction can make up for the tactile fun that shrinks when a pop-up book moves to a screen. Keep the advantage that digital storytelling makes possible, but treat everything below as a hypothesis.

### Why a pop-up book is fun (hypotheses to check)

- The surprise of the moment it unfolds.
- The hand movements of pulling, lifting and folding, and the instant response.
- Depth and dimension: layers stacked on layers.
- The sound and feel of paper.
- Turning pages together with an adult.

### Interaction ideas for the digital version

| Fun in the paper book | Idea for digital | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pulling a tab | Dragging with a finger stretches like a rubber band and snaps back, and pulling all the way makes something pop out | Check whether the child finds the drag easy |
| Opening a flap | A light lift shows what is underneath, and letting go closes it again with a sound | Let the story continue even if the flap is not opened |
| Things that rise up when unfolded | An animation where layers rise in order as the page turns, and a parallax effect where tilting the device shifts the layers | Keep it subtle so it does not cause dizziness |
| Paper sound and feel | Sound effects for turning and folding paper, and light vibration on devices that support it | Provide a volume control |
| Reading together | A split where a parent reads the text and the child touches the pictures, and large touch areas that two people can use on one screen | Make sure the adult does not block the child's control |
| Finding hidden things | One hidden picture on each page, and small surprises that respond when pressed | Make sure not finding it never blocks progress |
| Mixing paper and digital | Holding a phone or tablet over a physical pop-up book adds sound and motion (AR, or QR or NFC to play sound) | Costly and hard to make, so start by checking with paper and a video mockup |

### Examples tied to Korean topics

- **Ondol:** When the fire is lit in the furnace, a warm glow spreads across the floor. Express it with a dragging motion that gathers embers and a warm sound.
- **Giwa:** A scene where you place roof tiles one by one and a 3D roof takes shape.
- **Gwanghwamun:** A scene where unfolding the page opens the gate and the haetae pops out.

### Small experiments to run first

- In the paper vs. screen comparison, watch and note which actions the child responds to.
- Make the same scene as a paper cutout and as a screen animation and compare them. A low-cost mockup is enough.
- Check whether an adult's hand and the child's hand are used together.

## After the interviews

Treat the answers from two children and one set of parents as early evidence for the canvas, not proof. The goal is to confirm or overturn the guesses that would change the design the most.

### Turning answers into canvas changes

| If you hear or see this | Revise this box | Possible design change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The children are uncomfortable at stimulus step 2 | Child's Pains, Pain Relievers | Show only the whole gate and handle the missing pieces in a separate puzzle screen |
