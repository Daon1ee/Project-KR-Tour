# Interview Notes (English)

Oct 13, 2026 · @Daon

## 1. Interview Setup

- I used Claude to prepare the interview questions and question cards.
- The questions covered four main topics:
  1. **Korean identity**: How do parents plan to nurture it, and what does "Korean identity" mean to them? (the fundamental question)
  2. **Korean history**: The core of the interview. How do parents want to pass on dark and difficult history, how far are they comfortable going, and which periods do they want to teach?
  3. **Game style**: What young children enjoy, how they play, and parents' views on VR
  4. **Reactions to concept ideas**: From concepts for young children to how reactions change as children get older

## 2. Parent Interview (parents of three children, including a 6-year-old)

### ① Warm-up

- Weekends: They sometimes go outdoors. At home, one parent cares for a child while the other does housework. For comparison, I also asked another household (my relatives'), which is outdoorsy but sometimes stays home.
- Current favorite: Peppa Pig
- Fears: Even Pororo becomes scary when it turns dark or features villains.
- **Insight**: The children are relatively sensitive to unfamiliar things, and reactions to scary content vary with temperament. The youngest adapts to new things more easily than the twins.

### ② Korean Identity

- Daily Korean exposure: Korean spoken at home, holidays with grandparents, Korean food, and Korean school (Hangul and culture).
- Since the children attend American Pre-K, the parents rely on Korean school for identity-building. It teaches things parents wouldn't have thought of, like the Taegukgi, Shin Saimdang, and King Sejong.
- The parents don't want their children to lose their Korean identity, but they note the kids now ask a lot about English words and sentences. They seem slightly worried that exposure to American culture could lead to an exclusively American identity.
- The children are too young to distinguish American from Korean identity. The parents cited a video showing that very young children don't notice differences like disability or race. Recently, though, the children began asking, "We live in America, but we're Korean, right?"
- Priorities to pass on: **① language and food** (planting seeds through real cultural experience) → **② Korean manners** → **③ history and holidays** (too abstract, so last)
- Building identity hasn't felt difficult so far. It comes naturally because the parents enjoy Korean culture themselves, and Korean school helps a lot. Abstract things like Gwanghwamun and the King Sejong statue are hard to explain in words, so they plan to introduce them when the children are old enough to remember and grasp them.

### ③ Korean History

- The children are too young for dark history like the Japanese colonial period, but the parents believe they must learn it and plan to teach modern history later.
- For now (age 6), they want the children to learn about **great historical figures**. The parents take great pride in how many great figures came from such a small country, and hope their children build the same pride.
- They consider **middle to high school** the right time for dark history like the colonial period and the Korean War.
  - **Insight**: In Korea this is taught around upper elementary, but abroad there is far less media exposure. The parents don't want it taught casually and brushed off, and prefer to teach it properly when the child can understand rather than through constant exposure.
- If a game covers it, being **intuitive** matters most. They worry that simulations with branching choices let players clear one route and skip the others, which could **plant distorted history**.
- Their biggest worry is how the parents present it. If a debate arises with Japanese-heritage children, their kids should know accurate history well enough to argue against inaccuracies.
- **Key insight**: A 6-year-old's **attention span is at most 10 minutes**, so a game should be short, around 5-10 minutes.
  - The parents want to minimize media exposure.
  - Intuitive designs hold interest best. Kids want to touch and play with physical objects, while interest in media can fade naturally.

### ④ Platform and Game Style

- The parents avoid games as much as possible. If any, they prefer board games, or digital games where the child's real movement appears on screen and involves physical activity. The kids watch about an hour of TV on weekends.
- With new games, kids like listening to simple rules but learn harder ones by imitating.
- At children's museums, they love **intuitive things they can see and touch**. Shiny, sparkly VR-style visuals are comparatively less engaging.
- They don't want their children to try VR yet, and would start games (e.g., Roblox) around elementary school, since friends will be playing.
- Comparing the same story in digital vs. physical form, digital is fine for adults, but the parents kept stressing intuitiveness.
- Combining a paper book with a digital screen might even improve focus, and physical materials would draw interest first.

### ⑤ Reactions to Concept Ideas

- I heard both the parents' own opinions and reactions concerning the 6-year-old.
- Concepts tested:
  1. Story-choice simulation
  2. Restoration/collection puzzle
  3. Clue-and-record detective game (Clue-style, with the toddler Clue the kids currently play shown as an example)
  4. VR experience walking through the era and place
  5. Resource-collecting strategy game
  - **Simulation was poorly received, while the puzzle was met with real interest.** VR got a "we'd like to see it in person" response.
- Toddler concepts:
  1. Finding missing pieces with a guide character (puzzle-like)
  2. **Korean history through objects**: well received as intuitive and appealing to kids. If food appears, the game experience can feed into Korean identity when they later eat it for real
  3. 3D storybook

## 3. Third-Party Interview: The Children's Maternal Uncle

### ① Korean History

- Teaching Korean history to 6-year-olds is good in itself, but the colonial period and the Korean War are too early.
- Some history is fun and easy to approach, like hero tales (Yi Sun-sin, King Gwanggaeto). His examples:
  - Wang Geon: a drama that makes the tragedy of war entertaining through action, even though it's about people of the same nation fighting
  - Gaksital: torture scenes must be avoided, but it heroizes the colonial period and is fun
  - Hong Gil-dong, and Mr. Sunshine (romance drama first, historical action later)
- He suggests starting the colonial period and the Korean War in **upper elementary to middle school**: easier content around age 10, violent content at 13+. Before 6, kids could meet ondol, food, and holidays, then palaces, then later history.
- Personal experience: A WWII cartoon on the Tooniverse channel was boring and incomprehensible to him as a child, and he never watched it twice.
  - **Insight**: Difficult, boring content only brings boredom and has no staying power.
- He watched Wang Geon in 1st-2nd grade and wasn't scared even by an eye-gouging scene, but was scared by horror animation like school ghost stories.

### ② Games for Adults

- Gaksital, 731 Unit films, Mr. Sunshine, and Taegukgi (Brotherhood of War) made a fresh, lasting impression when he saw them as a teen, though violence can be heavy at that age.
- VR can make a 6-year-old dizzy or nauseous.
- He prefers collection puzzles, and also strategy simulations like Romance of the Three Kingdoms and Imjin War. On whether they'd be boring: it depends on the game's quality, and since the point is learning through fun, it doesn't matter. Even a game like Civilization isn't real history but teaches about civilizations indirectly, so what matters is learning something.
- If VR, he'd want a shooter set in the Korean War (13+).
- Media comparison (tablet / TV / PC / book)
  - **A book best stimulates imagination**, and he'd prefer a book if possible.
  - Clickable 3D objects would work better than a pop-up book.

## 4. My Comment

Through these interviews, I'm considering an **intuitive puzzle game**. Players assemble 3D objects piece by piece to restore the real form, and completing the puzzle opens up the related history. I'm considering both digital and physical puzzle versions, possibly a hybrid.
